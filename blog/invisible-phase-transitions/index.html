Probing invisible phase transitions via denoising score matching

Last edited 2026-11-21

TL;DR A large class of phase transitions in statistical mechanics is marked by a sudden change in the conditional correlation structure, while ordinary correlation functions show no signature of it. Conditional correlations are hard to detect from data, which makes these transitions effectively invisible. We show how score-matching techniques from machine learning can probe the conditional correlation structure, enabling unsupervised discovery of the phase diagram.

A central goal of statistical mechanics is to understand the different phases of matter. Colloquially speaking, a phase is characterized by the pattern of correlations among its many degrees of freedom. Traditionally the focus has been on few-point correlation functions, for at least two reasons. First, they are enough to characterize most equilibrium phase transitions. Second, physics is driven by experiment, both numerical and real, and few-point correlations are easy to estimate from data.

However, simple correlation functions do not always reveal the full correlation structure of a state. A case that has recently drawn growing attention is the class of noise-induced transitions,1 non-equilibrium phase transitions in which accumulating noise destroys an ordered state of matter. Such a transition leaves no signature in ordinary correlation functions. Instead, it is marked by a sudden change in the conditional correlation structure.

Concretely, partition the system into three regions \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), with \(B\) separating \(A\) from \(C\) (Fig. 1), and ask how \(A\) and \(C\) are correlated once \(B\) is given. In information theory, a natural measure of this is the conditional mutual information (CMI), \[I(A:C|B)=\sum_x p(x) \log\frac{p(x_A,x_C|x_B)}{p(x_A|x_B) p(x_C|x_B)},\] where \(p(x)\) is the probability distribution of configurations \(x\), and \(x_A\), \(x_B\), \(x_C\) are the restrictions of \(x\) to the three regions.

Intuitively, the CMI measures the locality of correlations. If the CMI drops quickly as \(B\) widens, a thin buffer around \(A\) already accounts for all of \(A\)’s correlations with the rest of the system. (This does not require the correlation between \(A\) and \(C\) itself to be small.)

Near a noise-induced transition, conditional correlations become long-ranged, a hallmark of the phase boundary between distinct phases. Away from the transition, by contrast, the CMI typically drops quickly to zero as \(B\) widens.

The picture is clear in theory, but it poses a problem for experiments and simulations: an ordinary correlation can be measured directly from data, whereas a conditional correlation cannot, which makes these transitions hard to detect.2 So the question we aim to address is:

Given a sufficient supply of data samples, how can we probe conditional correlations in the data?

Remark. Without any structure imposed on the data, the data distribution should be very hard to learn. For the distributions that arise in statistical mechanics, however, we are more confident that a method exists, because these distributions are far more structured. The decay of conditional correlations is itself one such structure: even in the so-called long-range regime, the CMI still decays as a power law in distance. There may also be structures we do not yet understand. Our hope, admittedly wishful, is that modern deep-learning methods, which we adapt below, can exploit all of this structure and make learning feasible.

The relation therefore goes both ways:

Here we focus on the probing direction, although how modern deep-learning methods exploit the structure in data is an equally, if not more, interesting question.

1. Score matching methods for probing conditional correlations

Our approach is based on score matching. Introduced in Hyvärinen 2005 as a way to fit a model distribution without computing its partition function, the method has gained wide popularity through its role in diffusion models, today one of the most widely used approaches to image generation.

1.1 The score function and conditional correlations

For concreteness, from now on we consider a two-dimensional \(L\times L\) lattice with a discrete variable \(x_i\) on every site \(i\), and let \(p(x)\) be the probability distribution of configurations.

The score function is defined as \[S(x)_i:=\log p(x_i|x_{\setminus i}),\] where \(x_{\setminus i}\) denotes all variables except \(x_i\). In the language of statistical physics, view \(p\) as the Gibbs state of a Hamiltonian, \(p(x)=e^{-H(x)}/Z\), and write \(H(x)=\sum_X h_X(x_X)\) as a sum of terms acting on subsets \(X\) of sites. Terms that do not contain \(i\) cancel in the conditional distribution, so \[S(x)_i=-H_i(x)-\log\sum_{x_i'}e^{-H_i(x_i', x_{\setminus i})},\qquad H_i:=\sum_{X\ni i}h_X.\] Up to a normalization over the single variable \(x_i\), the score at \(i\) is the part of the Hamiltonian that contains site \(i\).

How is the score related to conditional correlations? Return to the geometry of Fig. 1 and suppose that \(I(A:C|B)=0\), i.e. \(A\) and \(C\) are independent conditioned on \(B\). By the Hammersley–Clifford theorem (here simply the factorization \(p(x)=p(x_A,x_B) p(x_C|x_B)\)), \(H\) can then be written as a sum of terms none of which touches both \(A\) and \(C\). For every site \(i\in C\), the local Hamiltonian \(H_i\) therefore depends only on \(x_B\) and \(x_C\), and so does the score \(S(x)_i\). In other words, the score satisfies \[x_{\setminus A}=y_{\setminus A}\implies S(x)_C=S(y)_C,\] where \(x_{\setminus A}\) denotes \(x\) outside \(A\), and \(S(x)_C\) the scores at all sites of \(C\). For a positive distribution the converse also holds, so this property is equivalent to \(I(A:C|B)=0\). The score function therefore gives us a way to probe whether conditional correlations are long-ranged: change \(x\) in a small region, and measure how much the score changes as a function of the distance from that region.

We consider two deep-learning methods for estimating the score from data.

1.2 Direct score matching

The most direct approach represents the conditional distribution by a neural network \(f_\theta\) whose input and output are both \(L\times L\) arrays. At each site it outputs a distribution over the values of \(x_i\), \(f_\theta(x)_i=q_\theta(\cdot|x_{\setminus i})\). It is trained on samples by minimizing the cross-entropy summed over sites, \[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{PL}}(\theta)=-\mathbb E_{x\sim p}\sum_i\log q_\theta(x_i|x_{\setminus i}),\] which is the negative log-pseudolikelihood. Its optimum is \(q_\theta=p\), because each term decomposes as \(\mathbb E_{x\sim p}[-\log q_\theta(x_i|x_{\setminus i})] = \mathbb E_{x_{\setminus i}}[\mathbb H(p(\cdot|x_{\setminus i})) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\cdot|x_{\setminus i}) \| q_\theta(\cdot|x_{\setminus i}))]\). Only the KL divergence depends on \(\theta\), and it vanishes only when \(q_\theta\) equals the true conditional. The trained network then gives the score directly, \(S(x)_i\approx\log q_\theta(x_i|x_{\setminus i})\).

The difficulty lies in the architecture. The output at site \(i\) must be strictly independent of the input \(x_i\). Otherwise the network can simply copy \(x_i\) and drive the loss to zero, an information leak. If we only wanted the score at one site, this would be easy: hide \(x_i\) and predict it. But then each forward pass yields a single conditional, which is very inefficient in both data and compute. We want one network that outputs the scores of all sites at once, as an \(L\times L\) array, while every output is blind to its own site: a blind-spot network.

We meet this constraint with directional convolutions, following Laine et al. 2019 (Fig. 2). Unlike a conventional convolution layer, which receives information from all directions, a directional layer looks to one side only. The channels are grouped into four streams, left (L), right (R), up (U) and down (D), which are processed separately in the convolutional layers. In the L stream, for example, the kernel is designed so that the output at \((i,j)\) reads only columns \(j'\le j\) (Fig. 2a). Stacking \(n\) such layers grows the receptive field only to the left, and guarantees an invariant that holds at every layer: an L channel at site \((i,j)\) is a function only of the input in columns \(j'\le j\). The R, U and D streams are the same construction rotated by multiples of \(90^\circ\), with the corresponding invariants.

The prediction at \((i,j)\) combines the four streams, each read at the neighbours on its own side (Fig. 2c), using only pointwise layers. By the invariant, none of these features depends on the input at \((i,j)\), so nothing leaks.

In statistical mechanics, the data distribution usually comes with parameters, such as a temperature or a noise strength. The network must take them as inputs too. We feed them in through conditioning, a technique widely used in diffusion models, so that one network covers the whole parameter range.

1.3 Diffusion-model score matching

The second method is particularly suited to noise-induced transitions with i.i.d. noise. Suppose the data distribution has the form \[p_\alpha(x)=\sum_s p_0(s)\prod_i k_\alpha(x_i|s_i),\] i.e. a clean configuration \(s\sim p_0\) observed through a noise channel \(k_\alpha\) of strength \(\alpha\) that acts independently and identically on every site. Then we can train a network to denoise. Given the noisy \(x\) and \(\alpha\), it predicts at every site a distribution \(q_\theta(s_i|x,\alpha)\) over the clean value, by minimizing \[\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta)=-\mathbb E_{\alpha,s,x}\sum_i\log q_\theta(s_i|x,\alpha).\] Training needs only clean samples, since the noise is added synthetically.

Crucially, the prediction at site \(i\) may now depend on \(x_i\) itself, so any standard architecture works. We use a U-Net, a common diffusion-model backbone (for its architecture, see Ronneberger et al. 2015). Readers familiar with (discrete) diffusion models will recognize the objective, hence the name of the method. Our purpose, however, is not generative modeling.

As with the pseudolikelihood, the optimum is the true posterior, \(q_\theta(s_i|x,\alpha)=p_\alpha(s_i|x)\). The optimal denoiser determines the score of the noisy data exactly. Replacing \(x_i\) by \(x_i'\) changes only the factor \(k_\alpha(x_i|s_i)\), so \(p_\alpha(x_i',x_{\setminus i})/p_\alpha(x)=\mathbb E[k_\alpha(x_i'|s_i)/k_\alpha(x_i|s_i)\,|\,x]\). Summing over \(x_i'\) gives \[S(x)_i=-\log\sum_{s_i}\frac{p_\alpha(s_i|x)}{k_\alpha(x_i|s_i)},\] a discrete analogue of Tweedie’s formula.

1.4 Measuring the response

We now make the probe of Sec. 1.1 concrete. Given a sample \(x\) and a small region \(A\), we form a second input \(x'\) that agrees with \(x\) outside \(A\) and is again a typical sample. For data of the form of Sec. 1.3 this is simple: keep the clean configuration \(s\) behind \(x\) and redraw the noise inside \(A\), \(x_i'\sim k_\alpha(\cdot|s_i)\) independently for \(i\in A\). The response at a site \(i\notin A\) is \[r(A,i)=\mathbb E \|F(x)_i-F(x')_i\|^2,\] the mean squared change of the network output at \(i\), averaged over samples and noise. Here \(F\) is the native output of each network: the conditional probabilities \(q_\theta(\cdot|x_{\setminus i})\) for direct score matching, and the posterior \(q_\theta(s_i|x,\alpha)\) for denoising score matching.

To condense the spatial response into one number per parameter point, we use the distance-weighted average \[R_\gamma(A)=\frac{1}{|\bar A|}\sum_{i\notin A}d_i^\gamma r(A,i),\] where \(d_i\) is the distance from \(i\) to the centre of \(A\), and \(|\bar A|\) is the number of sites outside \(A\). The weight \(d^\gamma\) suppresses the trivial short-distance response and emphasizes slowly decaying tails. If \(r\sim d^{-\kappa}\), the sum grows with system size once \(\gamma>\kappa-2\), whereas an exponentially decaying response gives a finite \(R_\gamma\) for every \(\gamma\).

2. Example: the noisy Ising loop model

We benchmark both methods on a two-parameter family of loop configurations. Place a variable \(x_\ell\in\{\pm1\}\) on every link \(\ell\) of an \(L\times L\) periodic square lattice, and call a link occupied when \(x_\ell=-1\). The clean distribution \(x^{\beta,0}\) is a gas of closed loops (every vertex touches an even number of occupied links), penalized by their total length: \[p_{\beta,0}(x)\propto e^{-2\beta |x|},\qquad |x|=\#\{\ell: x_\ell=-1\}.\] This is the distribution of domain walls of the two-dimensional Ising model at inverse temperature \(\beta\). With Ising spins \(\sigma\) on the plaquettes and \(x_\ell=\sigma_p\sigma_q\) for the two plaquettes \(p,q\) adjacent to \(\ell\), the Ising weight is \(e^{\beta\sum_\ell\sigma_p\sigma_q}=e^{\beta(N-2|x|)}\), with \(N=2L^2\) links. The clean loops are therefore critical at the Ising point \(\beta_c=\tfrac12\ln(1+\sqrt2)\approx0.441\). The noisy family is obtained by flipping every link independently with probability \(\alpha\), \[x^{\beta,\alpha}=x^{\beta,0}\odot\eta^{\alpha},\qquad \Pr(\eta^\alpha_\ell=-1)=\alpha,\] which is exactly the setting of Sec. 1.3, with \(\alpha\) as the noise parameter.

The phase diagram of this model is known from Wiese et al. 2026 (Fig. 3a). The Ising line \(\beta=\beta_c\) is a conventional transition, visible in ordinary correlation functions. A second, hidden transition runs from \(\alpha_c^{(1)}\approx0.109\) at \(\beta=0\) to \(\alpha_c^{(2)}\approx0.201\) at \(\beta=\beta_c\). Across it the CMI becomes long-ranged, while ordinary correlations stay short-ranged.

We measure the response (Sec. 1.4) on the \(64\times64\) lattice, with \(A\) the central \(4\times4\) block of sites, together with their links, and \(d_i\) the periodic distance. We evaluate \(R_\gamma\) on a grid in the \((\alpha,\beta)\) plane and show \(\gamma=4\).

The method works reasonably well for locating the phase boundary, and the diffusion model does clearly better than direct score matching (Fig. 3). Quantitatively, however, the trained models are not yet good enough. The response profiles show no clear preference for power-law over exponential decay (Fig. 5), so at least for now we cannot use the method to extract critical properties. Taken together, the method gives a sketch of the phase diagram, not a quantitative analysis of it.

3. Limitations

  • ML-based methods in general. Optimization cannot make the network reproduce the score exactly. At best it approximates the score well on the typical set of the data distribution, and far from that set its output is uncontrolled. The response must therefore be evaluated on typical inputs only. This is why \(x'\) is formed by redrawing the noise inside \(A\) (Sec. 1.4): \(x'\) is then itself a typical sample.
  • Direct score matching. It does clearly worse than denoising score matching (Fig. 3). We attribute this to its four streams, which are processed separately and combined only in the final pointwise layers.
  • Denoising score matching. It applies only when the data are clean samples passed through known i.i.d. noise (Sec. 1.3). It also tends to predict poorly when the noise is weak, a known issue of denoising score matching.
